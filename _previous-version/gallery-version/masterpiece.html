My Favorite Masterpiece

Of all seven works, Homer’s Iliad stayed with me the longest. It looks like a poem about war, but underneath, it is about grief, pride, and the moment two enemies recognize each other as human.

“Sing, O goddess, the anger of Achilles son of Peleus.”

Homer, the Iliad, Book I, translated by Samuel Butler

The arc of a hero’s anger

The Iliad covers only a few weeks of a ten-year war, but it follows one emotion from beginning to end. Achilles’ rage starts as wounded pride, grows into a deadly grief after Patroclus dies, and finally breaks when an old man kneels in front of him and weeps.

That shape is what makes the poem unforgettable to me. It is not a story about winning a war. It is a story about a man learning, too late and at great cost, what his anger has done.

Four reasons this work stays with me

ΜΗΝΙΣmenis, rage

It is honest about anger

The very first word of the poem is “rage.” Homer does not pretend that heroes are calm. He shows how one insult can spread until it costs the lives of friends and strangers. I have felt anger take over a whole week of my life, so Achilles felt painfully real to me.

ΚΛΕΟΣkleos, glory

It questions what glory is worth

In Book 9, Achilles reveals that he has two possible fates: a short life with eternal glory, or a long life without fame. Every warrior in the poem is chasing glory, yet the poem keeps asking whether a name remembered forever is worth a life cut short.

ΟΙΚΟΣoikos, home and family

The “enemy” is human too

Homer gives the Trojans as much heart as the Greeks. Hector is a loving husband and father who fights to protect his city, not for fame. Because I came to care about him, his death hurt more than any battle scene could.

ΕΛΕΟΣeleos, pity

It ends with mercy, not victory

The poem does not end with the fall of Troy. It ends with Achilles and Priam, two enemies, weeping together over the people they have lost. Then Achilles gives back Hector’s body. After so much killing, that small act of kindness is the real climax.

Two faces of Achilles

Two painters, almost seventy years apart, captured the two sides of the same hero: the rage that drags a body through the dust, and the mercy that gives it back.

Matsch shows Achilles at his most terrifying, raising Hector’s helmet like a trophy. Ivanov shows him quiet and troubled, with an old man kissing his hand. Together they show what I love about the Iliad: the same person who is capable of great cruelty is also capable of great compassion, and the choice between them is his.

My favorite scene: Hector and Andromache

In Book 6, Hector briefly leaves the battle and meets his wife Andromache and their baby son Astyanax at the Scaean Gates of Troy. Andromache begs him not to go back, because she has already lost her father and brothers in the war. Hector answers that he would be ashamed to hide, even though he fears what will happen to her if Troy falls.

Then the baby sees his father’s bronze helmet with its nodding horsehair plume and starts to cry. Hector and Andromache laugh. Hector takes off the helmet, lays it on the ground, and lifts his son into his arms. For one moment, the greatest warrior of Troy is simply a father.

This scene stays with me because we know it is the last time they will be together. It shows what war really takes away: not just soldiers, but fathers, husbands, and the ordinary happiness of a family.

What it means to me

The Iliad taught me that anger is easy, but mercy takes courage. Achilles’ pride reminded me of how hiya and wounded pride can make us push away the people we need most. The poem ends with a funeral, much like our own lamay, where even old rivals come to grieve together. I want to remember Achilles in Book 24, not Book 22: the moment he chose compassion over revenge.
