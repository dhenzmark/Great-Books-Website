REFLECTIN

Looking back on this journey through seven great works, here is what changed in the way I read, think, and see the world.

Pictures above: Jacques-Louis David, Andromache Mourning Hector, 1783, and J. M. W. Turner, Ulysses Deriding Polyphemus, 1829.

ΨΥΧΗ

When I first saw the list of works for this course, I felt nervous. The titles sounded heavy and distant: epics from India, Greece, and France, and a book of the Bible I had only heard about in church. I expected long names, strange gods, and stories that had nothing to do with my life. I was ready to memorize facts, pass the exams, and move on.

What I found instead was a mirror. In Job, I saw the questions my own family asked after hard times: why us, and why now? In the Panchatantra, I recognized the classmates who spread gossip and the friends who stood by me. In the Ramayana and Mahabharata, I saw how heavily duty to family can weigh on a person, something every Filipino child understands. In the Iliad, I saw how pride and anger can destroy relationships. In the Odyssey, I felt the longing of families separated by distance. In The Song of Roland, I saw how hard it can be to ask for help.

The challenges

This was not always easy. The Mahabharata has so many characters that I had to draw a family tree just to keep track of who was fighting whom. The language of older translations was difficult, and I often read a passage two or three times before I understood it. I also struggled with parts of these works that conflicted with my values, such as the way some women are treated and the way the enemy is portrayed in The Song of Roland. Learning to appreciate a work while still questioning it was one of the most important skills I gained.

How I grew

The biggest change is in how I read. I used to read only to find out what happens next. Now I slow down and ask why a character makes a choice, what a symbol might mean, and what the story says about the people who told it. Looking at paintings of these stories, from Persian miniatures to Victorian canvases, also taught me that every age reads the classics in its own way.

I also became more patient with difficulty. Krishna’s advice to Arjuna, to do your duty without clinging to the results, helped me during stressful weeks. I focused on understanding each work rather than worrying only about my grade, and I enjoyed the work more because of it.

Finally, I gained a deeper appreciation for my own culture. Learning that the Ramayana lives on in the Maranao Maharadia Lawana, and that Charlemagne’s knights inspired our moro-moro plays, showed me that the Philippines has always been part of a larger world of stories. Our own epics, like Biag ni Lam-ang and Hinilawod, deserve the same careful attention I gave to Homer and Valmiki.

What I will carry forward

These stories have lasted for thousands of years because they speak to something that does not change: the human heart. People still struggle with suffering, still argue about duty, still get angry, still long for home, and still wonder whether courage means fighting alone or asking for help.

If my favorite work, the Iliad, taught me anything, it is that anger is easy, but mercy takes courage. Like Achilles in the final book, I want to choose compassion even when pride tells me otherwise. This portfolio marks the end of one journey, but I hope it is the beginning of a lifelong habit of reading, questioning, and finding myself in the stories of others.

Dhenz Mark A. Alden

4th Year BS Information Technology, GEC-GB-NRn 1454
