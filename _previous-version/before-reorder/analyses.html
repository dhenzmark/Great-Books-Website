Voice out of the Whirlwind

איוב

c. 6th–4th century BCE

Source
The Book of Job, chapters 38–42
Author
Unknown
Tradition
Hebrew wisdom literature
Form
Poetic dialogue in a prose frame

A righteous man loses everything, demands an answer from God, and receives a storm of questions instead.

  • Innocent suffering
  • Limits of knowledge
  • Honest faith

The Book of Job opens in the court of heaven. God praises Job as a blameless and upright man, but the Adversary (in Hebrew, ha-satan) argues that Job is faithful only because he is rich and protected. God allows Job to be tested. In a single day Job loses his oxen, sheep, camels, servants, and all ten of his children. He is then struck with painful sores from head to foot. Even so, he refuses to curse God.

Three friends, Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar, come to comfort him. They sit with him in silence for seven days, but when they finally speak, they insist that Job must have sinned, because God only punishes the guilty. Job rejects this. He knows he is innocent, and he demands a hearing before God. A younger man, Elihu, adds his own speech. Then God finally answers Job “out of the whirlwind.”

God does not explain why Job suffered. Instead, God asks a flood of questions: Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth? Can you command the morning or feed the young lions? God describes the wild ass, the ostrich, the war horse, the hawk, and finally two great creatures, Behemoth and Leviathan, that no human can tame. Job is humbled. He says that before he had only heard of God, but now he has seen Him. God rebukes the three friends for not speaking rightly, and Job’s fortunes are restored twice over.

  • JobA wealthy, righteous man whose faith is tested by total loss. He is honest about his anger and pain.
  • GodThe Creator, who speaks from the storm with questions rather than answers.
  • The AdversaryA member of the heavenly court who challenges whether Job’s goodness is genuine.
  • The FriendsEliphaz, Bildad, Zophar, and later Elihu, who defend a simple rule that suffering always means sin.
  • Innocent sufferingThe book refuses the easy idea that bad things only happen to bad people.
  • The limits of human knowledgeGod’s questions show how small human understanding is next to the vastness of creation.
  • Honest faithJob argues, complains, and questions, yet God says Job spoke rightly. Faith can include struggle.
  • False comfortThe friends mean well, but their explanations wound Job more than their silence did.

The book uses a frame structure: a simple prose story at the beginning and end surrounds a long and complex poem in the middle. The whirlwind speeches rely on rhetorical questions, which do not expect answers but overwhelm the listener with wonder. The poetry uses parallelism, a feature of Hebrew verse in which a second line repeats or expands the first, as in “Hast thou entered into the springs of the sea? or hast thou walked in the search of the depth?” Vivid imagery of animals and storms turns the speech into a tour of creation. The story is also full of dramatic irony: the reader knows about the heavenly test, but Job and his friends never learn of it.

Job belongs to the wisdom literature of the Hebrew Bible, alongside Proverbs and Ecclesiastes. Much of that tradition teaches that the wise and righteous prosper. Job challenges that teaching from the inside. Scholars believe it was written by an unknown poet, possibly during or after the Babylonian exile, when the people of Israel were asking why a faithful nation had suffered so much. The book has inspired writers, philosophers, and artists for over two thousand years, including William Blake, whose illustrations appear on this page.

My Insight

What surprised me most was that God never gives Job a reason. At first this felt unfair. Then I realized the speech is not an answer but a change of view. Job stops seeing only his own pain and starts seeing how large the world is. In the Philippines, we often face typhoons, floods, and losses we did not cause. Job taught me that I do not always need an explanation to keep going, and that sitting quietly with someone who is hurting may help more than explaining their pain.

The Panchatantra

पञ्चतन्त्र

c. 200 BCE – 300 CE

Attributed to
Vishnu Sharma
Language
Sanskrit
Meaning
“Five Treatises”
Form
Frame tale of animal fables in prose and verse

A wise teacher turns three dull princes into thinkers in six months, using nothing but stories about animals.

  • Practical wisdom
  • Friendship
  • Brains over strength

The Panchatantra begins with a frame story. King Amarashakti is worried because his three sons are dull and refuse to learn. A wise old Brahmin named Vishnu Sharma promises to make the princes wise in the ways of the world in only six months. Instead of lecturing, he teaches through stories, mostly about animals who talk, scheme, and make mistakes the way people do.

The work has five books. The Loss of Friends tells how two jackals, Karataka and Damanaka, destroy the friendship between Pingalaka the lion and Sanjivaka the bull through gossip and lies. The Winning of Friends shows a crow, a mouse, a tortoise, and a deer who save one another through loyalty and teamwork. Crows and Owls describes a war between the two birds, won through clever strategy. Loss of Gains includes the famous tale of the monkey who escapes a crocodile by quick thinking. Ill-Considered Action warns against haste, as in the story of the Brahmin’s wife who kills a loyal mongoose before learning it had saved her baby from a snake.

Stories are nested inside other stories. A character often begins a tale to prove a point, and within that tale another character tells yet another. The result is a web of lessons that the princes, and the reader, must learn to connect.

  • Vishnu SharmaThe wise teacher and narrator of the frame story.
  • DamanakaAn ambitious jackal who breaks a friendship for his own gain.
  • The Four FriendsThe crow, mouse, tortoise, and deer, who show the power of loyalty.
  • The MonkeyA clever animal who escapes a crocodile with a quick lie about leaving his heart in a tree.
  • Practical wisdom (niti)The book teaches how to live wisely in a real and often dangerous world.
  • Friendship and betrayalTrue friends protect each other, while flatterers bring ruin.
  • Brains over strengthSmall, weak animals often defeat stronger ones by thinking clearly.
  • Think before actingMany tragedies in the book come from haste and anger.

The most important device is the frame narrative, a story that holds other stories, used again and again in layers. The animals are examples of anthropomorphism, which gives animals human speech and personality. Each tale works as a fable that ends with a clear moral. The prose is mixed with short verses that state a lesson in memorable form, much like our own salawikain. The book is also full of irony, since clever characters are often trapped by their own tricks.

The Panchatantra belongs to the Indian tradition of nitishastra, writing about wise conduct and statecraft. It was meant for future rulers, but its lessons fit anyone. It became one of the most widely traveled books in history. It was translated into Middle Persian in the sixth century, then into Arabic as Kalila wa Dimna, and from there into Hebrew, Latin, and many European languages. Both paintings in this analysis come from Persian and Arabic versions, which shows how far the stories traveled.

My Insight

The Panchatantra reminded me of the fables I heard in elementary school, like the story of the monkey and the turtle. I liked how it treats wisdom as something practical. The tale of Damanaka made me think about how gossip can ruin friendships, even in a classroom or group chat. The four friends reminded me of bayanihan, the Filipino spirit of helping one another. Their story shows that ordinary creatures can survive almost anything if they work together.

The Ramayana

रामायण

c. 5th century BCE – 1st century CE

Attributed to
Valmiki
Language
Sanskrit
Meaning
“Rama’s Journey”
Form
Epic of about 24,000 verses in seven books

A prince gives up his throne to keep his father’s word, then crosses an ocean to rescue his wife.

  • Dharma
  • Good versus evil
  • Loyalty

The Ramayana tells the story of Prince Rama of Ayodhya, the eldest son of King Dasharatha, who is loved by everyone. Rama wins the hand of Princess Sita by lifting and breaking a mighty bow that no other suitor could even move. Just before Rama is to be crowned, his stepmother Kaikeyi reminds the king of two wishes he once promised her. She demands that her own son, Bharata, become king and that Rama be sent into the forest for fourteen years. Bound by his word, Dasharatha agrees, and he soon dies of grief.

Rama accepts his exile without complaint. Sita and his loyal brother Lakshmana insist on going with him. Bharata refuses to take the throne and rules only as Rama’s representative, placing Rama’s sandals on the throne. In the forest, the demon king Ravana of Lanka tricks Rama and Lakshmana with a golden deer and carries Sita away. The vulture king Jatayu tries to stop him and is mortally wounded.

Searching for Sita, Rama allies with the monkey king Sugriva and his devoted general Hanuman. Hanuman leaps across the ocean to Lanka, finds Sita, and brings back word of her. An army of monkeys and bears builds a bridge to the island. In a great war, Rama kills Ravana and rescues Sita. Because she lived in another man’s palace, Sita undergoes a trial by fire to prove her purity, and the fire god returns her unharmed. Rama returns to Ayodhya and is crowned, beginning a golden age of just rule.

  • RamaThe ideal prince and an avatar of the god Vishnu, who always chooses duty over desire.
  • SitaRama’s faithful wife, admired for her courage, loyalty, and inner strength.
  • LakshmanaRama’s devoted younger brother and constant companion.
  • HanumanThe powerful monkey general, a symbol of selfless devotion.
  • RavanaThe ten-headed king of Lanka, learned and mighty but ruined by pride and desire.
  • Dharma (duty)Every character is judged by whether they fulfill their proper duty as a son, wife, brother, or king.
  • Good versus evilThe conflict between Rama and Ravana represents the victory of righteousness over pride.
  • Loyalty and devotionLakshmana, Bharata, Sita, and Hanuman each show a different kind of faithfulness.
  • The cost of promisesDasharatha’s word, once given, cannot be broken, even when keeping it destroys him.

The Ramayana is written mostly in the shloka, a verse form that tradition says Valmiki invented when grief over a slain bird burst from him as poetry. It uses archetypes: the perfect hero, the faithful wife, the loyal servant, and the proud villain. The forest works as a symbol of testing, a place outside ordinary life where character is proven. Supernatural elements, such as shape-shifting demons and a flying monkey, are woven into a moral universe. The golden deer is an example of foreshadowing, since a beautiful illusion leads to great loss.

The Ramayana is one of the two great Sanskrit epics of India, and it remains a living part of Hindu worship, festivals, and theater. The festival of Diwali celebrates Rama’s return to Ayodhya. The story spread across Southeast Asia, where it was adapted into versions such as the Ramakien of Thailand and the Hikayat Seri Rama of Malaysia. It even reached the Philippines: the Maranao people of Mindanao have their own version, Maharadia Lawana, in which Ravana becomes a central figure.

My Insight

Rama’s choice to accept exile was hard for me to understand at first. He valued his father’s word more than his own comfort, which reminded me of utang na loob and how deeply we feel duty to our parents. I also found Sita’s trial by fire troubling, because it shows how societies often place a heavier burden of proof on women. Learning that the Maranao have their own version made this epic feel close to home.

The Mahabharata

महाभारत

c. 400 BCE – 400 CE

Attributed to
Vyasa
Language
Sanskrit
Length
About 100,000 couplets in eighteen books
Contains
The Bhagavad Gita

Two branches of one royal family go to war, and on the eve of battle a warrior asks his god what duty really means.

  • Complex duty
  • Family conflict
  • Cost of war

The Mahabharata is often called the longest poem in the world. At its center is a struggle between two branches of the royal family of Hastinapura. The five Pandava brothers, Yudhishthira, Bhima, Arjuna, Nakula, and Sahadeva, are the sons of King Pandu. Their one hundred cousins, the Kauravas, are led by the jealous Duryodhana, eldest son of the blind king Dhritarashtra.

Duryodhana resents the Pandavas from childhood and tries many times to destroy them. Arjuna wins the princess Draupadi in an archery contest, and she becomes the wife of all five brothers. Later, Duryodhana’s uncle Shakuni challenges Yudhishthira to a rigged game of dice. Yudhishthira gambles away his kingdom, his brothers, himself, and finally Draupadi, who is dragged into the royal hall and humiliated. The Pandavas must then spend twelve years in exile and a thirteenth year in disguise.

When they return, Duryodhana refuses to give back even five villages. On the battlefield of Kurukshetra, Arjuna sees his teachers, cousins, and elders in the opposing army and loses the will to fight. His charioteer, Krishna, an avatar of Vishnu, speaks to him about duty, the soul, and devotion. This dialogue is the Bhagavad Gita. The war lasts eighteen days and kills nearly everyone. The Pandavas win, but the victory feels hollow, and the epic ends with their final journey toward heaven.

  • YudhishthiraThe eldest Pandava, known for honesty, but weakened by his love of gambling.
  • ArjunaThe great archer, whose doubts on the battlefield lead to the Bhagavad Gita.
  • KrishnaArjuna’s friend, guide, and charioteer, who reveals himself as divine.
  • DraupadiThe Pandavas’ wife, whose public humiliation becomes a cause of the war.
  • DuryodhanaThe proud Kaurava prince, driven by envy of his cousins.
  • KarnaA tragic warrior who, unknown to all, is the Pandavas’ eldest brother, yet fights loyally for Duryodhana.
  • The complexity of dharmaDuty is rarely simple. Good people often face choices where every option causes harm.
  • Family conflictThe greatest war in the epic is fought between cousins.
  • Action without attachmentKrishna teaches that one should do one’s duty without being controlled by desire for results.
  • The cost of warEven the winners lose almost everything they love.

The epic uses a frame narrative with several layers of storytellers. It is told by the bard Vaishampayana at a great snake sacrifice and retold by Ugrashravas to sages in a forest. It contains many embedded stories, such as the tale of Savitri and Satyavan. The Bhagavad Gita is a philosophical dialogue placed at the moment of greatest tension, just before battle. Characters like Karna are tragic figures, admirable people destroyed by loyalty and fate. The work is also full of moral ambiguity, since even the heroes win by breaking the rules of fair combat.

The Mahabharata grew over many centuries as storytellers added material. It is both a story and a library of religion, law, politics, and philosophy. The Bhagavad Gita is one of the most important texts of Hinduism and has influenced thinkers around the world, including Mahatma Gandhi. In the 1580s the Mughal emperor Akbar had it translated into Persian as the Razmnama, the “Book of War,” and its painted pages are now treasured in museums.

My Insight

This was the most challenging work for me because there is no clean line between good and bad. I felt sorry for Karna, who fought on the “wrong” side out of loyalty to the only friend who respected him. Krishna’s teaching helped me as a student, because I often worry so much about grades that I forget to focus on learning. The family torn apart over land and pride also reminded me of real inheritance disputes I have heard about.

The Iliad

ΙΛΙΑΣ

c. 8th century BCE

Attributed to
Homer
Language
Ancient Greek
Form
Epic in 24 books, dactylic hexameter
Setting
The tenth year of the Trojan War

The greatest warrior of the Greeks is insulted, refuses to fight, and learns too late what his anger costs.

  • Rage
  • Honor and glory
  • Shared humanity

The Iliad takes place during the tenth and final year of the Trojan War, which began when the Trojan prince Paris carried off Helen, wife of the Spartan king Menelaus. Rather than tell the whole war, the poem focuses on a few weeks and on one emotion: the rage of Achilles, the greatest Greek warrior.

Agamemnon, leader of the Greek army, is forced to give up a captive woman, so he takes Achilles’ prize, Briseis, instead. Insulted, Achilles withdraws from battle and asks his mother, the sea goddess Thetis, to make the Greeks suffer until they honor him. Without Achilles, the Greeks are driven back to their ships by the Trojan prince Hector.

Achilles’ closest friend, Patroclus, borrows his armor and leads a counterattack, but Hector kills him. Achilles’ anger now turns against Hector. He returns to battle, kills Hector outside the walls of Troy, and drags the body behind his chariot for days. In the final book, old King Priam secretly enters the Greek camp and begs for his son’s body. Achilles, remembering his own father, weeps with Priam and returns the body. The poem ends with Hector’s funeral, not with the fall of Troy.

  • AchillesThe greatest warrior of the Greeks, proud, passionate, and fated to die young.
  • HectorThe Trojan prince and defender of his city, a loving husband and father.
  • AgamemnonThe commander of the Greeks, whose arrogance starts the quarrel.
  • PatroclusAchilles’ gentle companion, whose death changes everything.
  • PriamThe aged king of Troy, whose courage in grief moves even Achilles.
  • Rage and its costAchilles’ anger brings death to his allies, his friend, and finally his enemy.
  • Honor and glory (kleos)Warriors fight for a name that will be remembered after death.
  • MortalityEvery hero must die, which makes each life and each choice more precious.
  • Shared humanityThe meeting of Priam and Achilles shows that enemies share the same grief.

The poem begins in medias res, in the middle of the action, and opens with an invocation to the Muse. Homer uses epithets, repeated descriptive phrases such as “swift-footed Achilles” and “Hector, breaker of horses,” which helped oral poets compose and audiences remember. Extended similes compare battle scenes to storms, fires, and lions attacking cattle, bringing the peaceful world into the violent one. The gods act as divine machinery, taking sides and interfering. Homer also gives brief histories of minor soldiers at the moment they die, so that no death is meaningless.

The Iliad came out of a long tradition of oral poetry in which singers performed stories from memory. It is one of the oldest works of Western literature. For the ancient Greeks, it was almost a textbook of values, teaching courage, honor, and hospitality. The story of the Trojan War has inspired painters, playwrights, and filmmakers for nearly three thousand years, as the gallery below shows.

My Insight

I expected the Iliad to celebrate war, but it actually mourns it. The scene that affected me most was Hector saying goodbye to his wife and baby son, who is frightened by his father’s helmet. Hector laughs, takes it off, and holds the child. Achilles showed me how wounded pride, or hiya, can make a person hurt everyone around them. His reconciliation with Priam taught me that compassion can exist even between enemies. This is my favorite work, and I explain why on the Masterpiece page.

The Odyssey

ΟΔΥΣΣΕΙΑ

Late 8th century BCE

Attributed to
Homer
Language
Ancient Greek
Form
Epic in 24 books, dactylic hexameter
Journey
Ten years from Troy to Ithaca

A clever king fights gods, monsters, and his own pride to get home, while his family holds on without him.

  • Homecoming
  • Cleverness
  • Hospitality

The Odyssey follows Odysseus, king of Ithaca, on his ten-year journey home after the Trojan War. When the poem begins, he has been held for seven years on the island of the nymph Calypso. Meanwhile, more than a hundred suitors have taken over his palace, eating his food and pressuring his wife, Penelope, to remarry. His son, Telemachus, now a young man, sets out to find news of his father.

The gods finally free Odysseus. Shipwrecked among the Phaeacians, he tells the story of his adventures. He blinded the Cyclops Polyphemus, a son of Poseidon, after tricking him by saying his name was “Nobody.” His men opened a bag of winds and blew their ships off course. The witch Circe turned his crew into pigs. He traveled to the underworld to consult the dead prophet Tiresias. He heard the song of the Sirens while tied to the mast, sailed between the monster Scylla and the whirlpool Charybdis, and lost his last companions after they ate the sacred cattle of the sun god.

The Phaeacians carry Odysseus home. Disguised as a beggar by the goddess Athena, he reunites with Telemachus and tests the loyalty of his household. Penelope announces that she will marry whoever can string Odysseus’ great bow and shoot an arrow through twelve axe heads. Only the beggar succeeds. He reveals himself, and with Telemachus he kills the suitors. Penelope tests him one last time with the secret of their bed, carved from a living olive tree, and the couple is finally reunited.

  • OdysseusA clever, patient, and sometimes reckless hero who survives by his wits.
  • PenelopeHis faithful wife, who outwits the suitors by weaving and unweaving a burial shroud.
  • TelemachusTheir son, who grows from a boy into a man over the course of the poem.
  • AthenaThe goddess of wisdom, who guides and protects Odysseus.
  • PoseidonThe sea god, who punishes Odysseus for blinding his son.
  • Homecoming (nostos)The deepest desire in the poem is to return home and be recognized.
  • Cleverness (metis)Odysseus wins through intelligence and disguise more than strength.
  • Hospitality (xenia)Good hosts are rewarded, and those who abuse hospitality, like the suitors, are punished.
  • Loyalty and patiencePenelope, Telemachus, and old servants keep faith through twenty years of absence.

Like the Iliad, the Odyssey begins in medias res. Most of the famous adventures are told in a flashback, with Odysseus narrating his own story, which makes him an entertaining but not always reliable storyteller. The poem uses epithets such as “wise Penelope” and “Odysseus of many turns.” Disguise and recognition scenes create suspense in the second half. The journey can also be read as an allegory of life, with each island representing a different temptation or danger.

The Odyssey reflects the values of the early Greek world, especially the sacred duty of hospitality toward strangers. It gave us the word “odyssey,” meaning any long and difficult journey. Its pattern of a hero leaving home, facing trials, and returning changed has shaped countless later stories, from novels to modern films.

My Insight

The Odyssey made me think of the many Filipino families with a parent working abroad. Like Penelope and Telemachus, they wait for years, holding the household together while hoping for a homecoming. Odysseus is not perfect, and his pride causes years of suffering, but he never stops trying to get home. His story reminded me that patience and cleverness can matter as much as strength.

The Song of Roland

La Chanson de Roland

c. 1100 CE

Author
Unknown; the manuscript names a Turoldus
Language
Old French
Form
Chanson de geste of about 4,000 lines in laisses
Event
Battle of Roncevaux Pass, 778 CE

A proud knight refuses to call for help until it is too late, while a traitor in his own family sells him out.

  • Loyalty
  • Courage versus wisdom
  • Feudal honor

The Song of Roland is set during the wars of Charlemagne, emperor of the Franks, in Spain. After seven years of fighting, only the city of Saragossa, ruled by the Saracen king Marsile, remains unconquered. Marsile offers a false peace. Roland, Charlemagne’s nephew and greatest knight, proposes his stepfather Ganelon as the messenger to Marsile. Furious and afraid, Ganelon plots revenge. He arranges for Marsile to ambush the rear guard of the Frankish army, led by Roland, as it crosses the mountain pass of Roncevaux.

As the huge Saracen army approaches, Roland’s wise friend Oliver begs him three times to blow his horn, the Oliphant, to call Charlemagne back. Roland refuses, saying it would bring shame on himself and on France. The rear guard fights heroically, but one by one the twelve peers fall. Only when almost everyone is dead does Roland blow the horn, so hard that his temples burst. He tries to break his sword, Durendal, so that no enemy will take it, but it will not shatter. He dies facing Spain, offering his glove to God, and angels carry his soul to paradise.

Charlemagne returns, mourns his nephew, and takes revenge. God makes the sun stand still so he can pursue the enemy. He also defeats Baligant, the emir of Babylon, who has come to aid Marsile. Back in France, Ganelon is put on trial. His guilt is decided by a trial by combat, which Charlemagne’s champion Thierry wins, and Ganelon is executed.

  • RolandCharlemagne’s nephew, brave and loyal, but proud to the point of disaster.
  • OliverRoland’s best friend and brother-in-arms, the voice of good judgment.
  • CharlemagneThe aged emperor, presented as God’s chosen ruler.
  • GanelonRoland’s stepfather, whose treason causes the massacre.
  • Archbishop TurpinA warrior priest who fights and blesses the dying knights.
  • Loyalty and betrayalThe poem contrasts Roland’s faithfulness with Ganelon’s treason.
  • Courage versus wisdomRoland’s bravery and Oliver’s caution are both virtues, but they lead to different choices.
  • Feudal honorA knight’s reputation and his duty to his lord matter more than his life.
  • Holy warThe poem frames the conflict as Christianity against Islam, reflecting the attitudes of the Crusades era.

The poem is written in laisses, stanzas of varying length in which each line ends with the same vowel sound, a pattern called assonance. It uses parallel laisses, retelling the same moment several times from slightly different angles, such as the three times Oliver asks Roland to blow the horn. Objects like the sword Durendal and the Oliphant are symbols of knightly identity and pride. Roland and Oliver are drawn as foils, each making the other’s qualities clearer. The poem also uses hyperbole, with knights cutting through enemies and horses in a single stroke.

The Song of Roland is the oldest major surviving work of French literature. It is loosely based on a real event: in 778 CE, the rear guard of Charlemagne’s army was ambushed at Roncevaux. Historically, the attackers were Basques, not Saracens. Three centuries later, around the time of the First Crusade, poets turned this small defeat into a story of holy war. The legend of Charlemagne and his peers later reached the Philippines through Spanish colonization, where it influenced the komedya or moro-moro, folk plays about battles between Christians and Moors.

My Insight

Roland’s refusal to blow the horn frustrated me, because it cost the lives of his whole rear guard. Still, I understood the pressure he felt not to look weak. Many of us refuse to ask for help because of pride or fear of being judged. Oliver showed me that wisdom is also a form of courage. I also read the poem critically, because it portrays Muslims unfairly, and learning that our moro-moro plays came from this tradition made me think about how stories shape the way one group sees another.
