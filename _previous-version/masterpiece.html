Section III

My Favorite Masterpiece: The Odyssey

Of all seven works, Homer’s Odyssey stayed with me the longest. It is a story about monsters and gods, but underneath, it is a story about wanting to go home and being recognized by the people you love.

The Journey

Ten years, one destination

Odysseus leaves Troy as a victorious warrior and arrives in Ithaca as a beggar with nothing. Every stop on his journey takes something from him: his ships, his crew, his pride, and his sense of who he is. Yet each loss also teaches him patience and self-control.

By the time he reaches home, he is ready to do what the young warrior of Troy could not. He waits, watches, and acts only when the moment is right.

Why I Chose It

Four reasons this work stays with me

  1. It is a story about home

    Odysseus is offered immortality by Calypso, yet he chooses to go home to a rocky island and an aging wife. That choice moved me. It says that the ordinary life we share with family is worth more than any paradise. Many Filipinos know this feeling: the longing of a parent working overseas, or a student living far from the province, waiting for the day they can return.

  2. The hero wins with his mind

    Achilles and Roland win through strength, but Odysseus wins through cleverness, patience, and disguise. He escapes the Cyclops by calling himself “Nobody.” He survives the Sirens by planning ahead. As a student, I found it encouraging that the greatest hero in this story is the one who thinks.

  3. Penelope is a hero too

    Penelope is just as clever as her husband. For years she holds off more than a hundred suitors by weaving a shroud by day and secretly unraveling it by night. In the end, she even tests Odysseus with the secret of their bed. Her strength is quiet, but without it, Odysseus would have no home to return to.

  4. It shows that heroes make mistakes

    Odysseus is not perfect. His pride makes him shout his real name at the Cyclops, which brings Poseidon’s anger and years of suffering. His crew’s greed costs them their lives. I appreciated that the poem does not hide these failures. It suggests that growth comes from learning through hardship, not from being flawless.

My Favorite Scene

The old dog Argos

In Book 17, Odysseus finally walks toward his own palace, disguised as a ragged beggar. No one recognizes him. Lying on a pile of manure by the gate is Argos, the dog he raised as a puppy twenty years before. Argos is now old, neglected, and covered in fleas. When he hears his master’s voice, he wags his tail and drops his ears, but he is too weak to come closer. Odysseus cannot greet him without revealing himself, so he turns away and secretly wipes a tear. Then Argos dies, having seen his master one last time.

This short scene is my favorite in all seven works. After all the monsters and gods, the first one to recognize the hero is a loyal old dog. It shows that love and loyalty can outlast time, distance, and even disguise.

What It Means to Me

The Odyssey taught me that a journey is not only about where you are going but about who you become along the way. Odysseus returns home older, poorer, and humbler, yet wiser than when he left. My own studies feel like a smaller odyssey. There are “Sirens” of distraction, “Cyclopes” of difficult exams, and moments when I feel lost at sea. Like Odysseus, I want to keep my eyes on home: my family, my goals, and the person I hope to be.
