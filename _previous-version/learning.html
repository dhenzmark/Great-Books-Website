Section IV

Summary of Learning

These seven works came from different places and centuries, yet they kept answering the same human questions. This page gathers the most important ideas I learned and shows how the works connect.

Big Ideas

Six lessons across seven works

1

Literature mirrors culture

Each work shows what its people valued most. Greece prized honor and cleverness, India prized dharma, medieval France prized feudal loyalty, and the Hebrew tradition wrestled with faith and justice. Reading a culture’s stories is a way of meeting its people.

2

Heroism has many faces

Achilles is a hero of strength, Odysseus of wit, Rama of duty, Roland of loyalty, and Job of endurance. The Panchatantra even makes heroes of a mouse and a tortoise. I learned that there is no single definition of a hero.

3

Duty and desire collide

Rama gives up a throne for his father’s word. Arjuna must fight his own family. Achilles chooses a short, glorious life over a long, quiet one. These works show that the hardest choices are between two things we care about.

4

Pride brings downfall

Achilles’ rage, Roland’s refusal to blow the horn, Duryodhana’s envy, Ravana’s desire, and Odysseus’ boast to the Cyclops all lead to suffering. Across cultures, pride is the most common cause of tragedy.

5

Suffering can bring wisdom

Job, Odysseus, the Pandavas, and Priam all pass through great loss. None of the works says suffering is good, but many show that it can deepen understanding and compassion when it is faced honestly.

6

Stories travel

The Panchatantra crossed into Persia and Europe. The Ramayana became Maharadia Lawana in Mindanao. The tales of Charlemagne became the moro-moro plays of the Philippines. Great stories do not belong to one nation.

How Stories Are Told

What I learned about literary craft

Before this course, I read mainly for plot. Now I notice how a story is told. Many of these works began as oral literature, performed from memory long before they were written down. That explains features like Homer’s repeated epithets and the repeated laisses of The Song of Roland. They helped performers remember the story and helped listeners follow it.

I also learned to recognize common techniques across cultures. The frame narrative appears in Job, the Panchatantra, and the Mahabharata. Beginning in medias res is central to both Homeric epics. Divine intervention shapes events in nearly every work, from Athena guiding Odysseus to Krishna guiding Arjuna. Seeing these patterns helped me understand that storytellers in different parts of the ancient world solved similar problems in similar ways.

Finally, I learned to read critically. Some parts of these works, such as Sita’s trial by fire or the hostile portrayal of Muslims in The Song of Roland, reflect attitudes we should question today. Appreciating a masterpiece does not mean accepting everything in it.

Skills I developed

  • Close readingLooking carefully at a passage to see how word choice and imagery create meaning.
  • ComparisonConnecting works from different cultures through shared themes and techniques.
  • ContextUnderstanding how history and belief shape what a story says.
  • Personal responseLinking ancient stories to my own life and to Filipino culture.
